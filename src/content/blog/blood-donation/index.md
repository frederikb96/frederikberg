A standard whole-blood donation takes about one pint, roughly half a litre. The Stanford Blood Center puts that against an adult total of about ten pints: "The average adult has 10 pints of blood in their bodies, and whole blood donations are currently capped at one pint per person" (https://stanfordbloodcenter.org/pulse-spring23-what-really-happens-to-your-body-after-you-donate-blood-effects-risks-and-recovery/). The question is how much that tenth costs the body afterwards, and for how long.

The short answer is that the effect is real, small and mostly short. Peak endurance drops by a few per cent and is back after about two weeks, there is no measurable rise in infections among male donors, and the one thing that takes months is refilling iron stores.

## Circulation and blood volume

The fluid part of the blood recovers first. According to the Stanford Blood Center, plasma "will begin to replenish immediately and should be fully back to normal within a few days", while "it typically takes 4–8 weeks to replace all the red cells lost in a whole blood donation" (same page). Drinking enough after the donation is what speeds up the first part, since plasma is mostly water.

## Endurance and peak performance

A systematic review of 18 before-after studies found that 24 to 48 hours after a donation of 400–500 ml, haemoglobin concentration was 7 % lower, maximal oxygen uptake 7 % lower and maximal exercise capacity 10 % lower. The authors rate the underlying studies as "of low quality" and describe the effects as "small but potentially physiologically important reductions [...] in the first 2 days after the blood donation" (Van Remoortel et al. 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27807869/).

The time course is clearer in a Danish study of 19 healthy men. Peak oxygen uptake fell by 6.5 % and a time trial was 5.2 % slower on day 3, and "Both VO2peak and TT performance were back to baseline 14 days after blood donation", while haemoglobin took longer (Ziegler et al. 2015, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25512178/). A randomised trial of repeated donations in moderately trained men concluded that "Maximal, but not submaximal, endurance capacity was altered after blood donation" (Meurrens et al. 2016, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27873242/). Easy endurance training is therefore hardly affected; hard intervals and races in the two weeks after a donation are.

No study measuring strength after a donation was found. Short efforts depend little on oxygen transport, so a large effect would be surprising, but that is reasoning, not evidence.

## Infection risk

The worry of getting ill after donating has no support in the largest data set on the question. The Danish Blood Donor Study followed 94,628 donors and found that iron deficiency was associated with slightly more antibiotic prescriptions in women (hazard ratio 1.08), while it "was not associated with overall risk of infection for men". The authors conclude that "risk of infection should not be considered an apprehension regarding blood donation" (Brøns et al. 2025, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39807019/). That study looks at iron deficiency among donors, not at the days right after a donation; no large study of that narrower window was found, so the honest result is "no sign of a risk" rather than "risk ruled out".

## Iron is the slow part

Iron is the one resource a donation removes that takes months to come back. In a randomised trial with 215 donors after a 500 ml donation, "Without iron supplements, 67% of participants did not recover iron stores by 168 days" (Kiss et al. 2015, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25668261/). In the same trial, one low-dose iron tablet a day shortened the time to recover 80 % of the lost haemoglobin to about a month. Haemoglobin, which is checked before every donation, only falls once the stores are already low; ferritin shows the stores themselves, and a study of male donors recommends it as "a more adequate index to use for iron deficiency screening" (Yousefinejad et al. 2010, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20859513/). For someone donating once or twice a year this is a small risk; asking for a ferritin value now and then settles it.

## Claimed health benefits

Donors tend to be healthier than the general population before they ever donate, which makes many reported benefits of donation hard to interpret. A study of 0.6 million donors in China found this healthy donor effect clearly, with non-donors showing a 15 % higher rate of the studied diseases than matched donors before the first donation (Su et al. 2024, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38386403/). Lower blood pressure or fewer heart attacks among donors are therefore no good reason to donate; the reason is the recipient.

## When a donation has to wait (Germany)

German blood services defer donors under common rules. The deferral list of the blood donation service at Uniklinik Aachen, as one example, reads:

> nach fieberhaften Erkrankungen und/oder Durchfallerkrankungen unklarer Ursache für 4 Wochen, [...] nach einem unkomplizierten Infekt für 1 Woche.
>
> *— Uniklinik RWTH Aachen, Blutspendedienst, Ausschlussgründe von einer Blutspende — https://www.ukaachen.de/fileadmin/files/institute/transfusionsmedizin/Blutspendedienst/20250926_DM_Dokument_Rueckstellungsgruende_WEB.pdf*

(After illnesses with fever and/or diarrhoea of unclear cause, four weeks; after an uncomplicated infection such as a cold, one week.)

> Keine Rückstellung ist erforderlich nach Applikation von Tot- bzw. Toxoidimpfstoffen oder gentechnisch hergestellten Impfstoffen (zum Beispiel Poliomyelitis inaktiviert, Typhus inaktiviert, Diphtherie, Influenza, Cholera inaktiviert, Tetanus, FSME, Hepatitis A, SARS-CoV-2), sofern der Spender ohne klinische Symptome und bei Wohlbefinden ist.
>
> *— same document*

(No deferral after inactivated, toxoid or recombinant vaccines, for example influenza, tetanus, tick-borne encephalitis, hepatitis A or SARS-CoV-2, as long as the donor has no symptoms and feels well.) Live vaccines and the hepatitis B vaccine defer for four weeks (same document). The final decision is made by the doctor on site.

## What follows

A healthy person can donate without a lasting cost. The practical consequences are small: a quiet donation day with plenty to drink, no hard endurance session for a few days and no important endurance event in the two weeks after, and, for regular donors, a look at ferritin rather than haemoglobin alone.
