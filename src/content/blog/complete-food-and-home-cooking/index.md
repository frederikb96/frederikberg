<div class="tldr">

In short

- **On climate it is close.** A vegan complete food and a home-cooked vegan diet both land between 1 and 3 kg CO₂e per 2000 kcal, roughly one day of food. A meat-heavy diet is about 10.
- **Refining makes the footprint, not the factory.** Turning soybeans into protein isolate multiplies their footprint by six. Mixing the powders together is 0.2 % of the total.
- **The levers sit around the food.** Out-of-season greenhouse vegetables, food waste, the drive to the shop and the cooking energy move the number more than shake versus stew.
- **On health there is no long-term evidence.** No study has followed anyone living on a complete food for longer than four weeks.

</div>

Complete foods are powders formulated to be a whole diet: mixed with water they supply calories, protein, fat, fibre and the full set of vitamins and minerals. Huel, Jimmy Joy, Mana and Soylent are the best-known brands, and most of the European ones are vegan. Part of their appeal is environmental, since nothing spoils, portions are exact and nothing is cooked. This article compares them with a regional, seasonal, whole-food vegan diet cooked at home in Germany, and answers three questions:

- Which of the two has the smaller climate footprint, and why?
- What actually drives the footprint of a complete food?
- What is known about the health effects of living mainly on one?

Both sides are vegan on purpose. Dropping animal products is already the largest lever on a diet's footprint, so holding it fixed leaves the narrower question of whether the form the plants arrive in changes anything.

## 1. Why the comparison is harder than it looks

Food footprints come from life-cycle assessments, which add up the emissions of every step from the field to the plate. Two choices decide what such an assessment says, and there is little independent data to work with:

- **Per what.** A complete food is a dry powder at about 417 kcal per 100 g; fresh vegetables are mostly water. Per kilogram the powder looks about ten times better, purely because of the water. All figures here are therefore per 2000 kcal.
- **Where the accounting stops.** At the factory gate, at the supermarket shelf, or only after cooking and waste at home. Cooking, waste and the trip to the shop are shown as separate lines here, so a comparison can be redone at another boundary.
- **No independent assessment exists.** No peer-reviewed assessment of any complete-food product has been published. Huel publishes an absolute figure and has its emissions inventory checked, but not the method behind it [\[1\]](https://huel.com/pages/sustainability-page). Jimmy Joy reports only how much it has offset, which is a payment, not a measurement [\[2\]](https://jimmyjoy.com/pages/how-sustainable-is-jimmy-joy).

Because of that last point, every figure for a complete food in this article is either a manufacturer claim or a reconstruction from ingredient data, and each is labelled as one.

## 2. Refining is where the footprint comes from

The reconstruction takes one product, Jimmy Joy's Plenny Shake, and works out its recipe from the declared ingredient order and nutrition panel [\[3\]](https://world.openfoodfacts.org/product/8720165350995/plenny-shake-jimmy-joy). Verified emission factors for each ingredient from the CarbonCloud database then give the footprint:

- **About 1.0 kg CO₂e per 2000 kcal**, with a plausible range of 0.9 to 1.3.
- **The factory is almost free.** Mixing the powders, the step people picture as "industrial food", is 0.2 % of the total. The footprint is already in the ingredients when they arrive.
- **Refined ingredients are expensive.** A whole soybean carries 1.22 kg CO₂e per kilogram. As protein isolate it carries 7.23, and 84 % of that comes from processing energy rather than farming [\[4\]](https://apps.carboncloud.com/climatehub/product-reports/id/96565698940).

![Bar chart comparing crops with their refined forms in kg CO₂e per kg: soybean 1.22, soy protein isolate 7.23; rolled oats 1.20, hydrolysed oat flour 4.17; sunflower oil 1.73, oil dried into powder 3.13; vitamin and mineral premix 15.0.](./refining.png)

Grinding a grain into flour barely counts, because the whole seed is still there. Pulling the protein out of a bean and drying it back into a powder takes a lot of heat, and that energy is in the number whatever the field looked like. Two products in the same category can therefore differ from each other more than either differs from home cooking: one built mainly from flours, like the Plenny Shake, sits near the bottom of the range, one built around protein isolates and dried oil near the top. The vitamin and mineral mix is also not the rounding error it is taken for. At 15 kg CO₂e per kilogram it is more carbon-intensive than beef per kilogram, and at about 2 % of the product's weight it still contributes roughly a sixth of the ingredient footprint [\[5\]](https://apps.carboncloud.com/climatehub/product-reports/id/186640392289).

Huel's own figure works out at about 3 kg CO₂e per 2000 kcal, three times the reconstruction [\[6\]](https://de.huel.com/pages/huel-sustainability-nutrition-facts). A recipe built around isolates explains perhaps half of that gap. The rest most plausibly comes from counting the whole company, offices, travel and marketing included, and dividing by meals sold. That would make the manufacturer's number conservative rather than flattering, but without a published method it stays a guess.

## 3. The home-cooked side

Two model days of 2000 kcal each were built from the French Agribalyse 3.1 database: one optimised around German seasonal and stored winter produce, one reflecting an ordinary vegan shop with imported and greenhouse items. Including waste, cooking and shopping they come to about 2.0 and 2.6 kg CO₂e. Three results matter more than the totals:

- **Seasonality is large, and it is about heating, not distance.** An out-of-season tomato carries 3.4 times the footprint of a seasonal one. The difference is almost all in the heated greenhouse; transport barely changes [\[7\]](https://data.ademe.fr/datasets/agribalyse-31-synthese).
- **Food miles mislead.** For apples, transport is 64 % of the footprint, but the footprint is small to begin with. Growing plants is cheap in emissions, so transport looks large as a share and saves little in tonnes.
- **Cooking energy is the largest line outside the food.** About 0.57 kg a day, half of the food itself in the optimised day, and most published comparisons leave it out.

A single 200 g portion of winter tomato is the largest line in the ordinary day, larger than all the grain in it together. Cooking and waste, on the other hand, are where a powder mixed with cold water has a real structural advantage: nothing is heated, and a sealed pouch does not go off at the back of the fridge.

## 4. Side by side

Put next to each other, all vegan options fall into one band, and the meat-heavy diet falls far outside it:

![Bar chart of kg CO₂e per 2000 kcal: complete food reconstructed from ingredients 1.03, optimised regional and seasonal day 1.99, measured average UK vegan diet 2.47, ordinary vegan day 2.55, Huel's own figure 3.00, and for scale a measured high-meat diet at 10.24.](./side-by-side.png)

The measured reference values come from the actual diets of 55,504 people in the UK, each food linked to a large review of life-cycle studies [\[8\]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43016-023-00795-w). An average vegan diet comes to 2.47 kg CO₂e per 2000 kcal, a high-meat diet to 10.24. These measured figures stop at the supermarket shelf and include plant milks, coffee and snacks that a model day leaves out, so they sit somewhat above the modelled days. The conclusion does not depend on that detail. Going from a meat-heavy to any vegan diet is a factor of four. Every choice inside the vegan band is a factor of three at most, and much of that is uncertainty in the reconstruction rather than a real difference.

## 5. Health

On health the evidence is missing rather than balanced:

- **No long-term study exists.** No trial or cohort has followed healthy adults getting most of their calories from a complete food for longer than four weeks.
- **"Ultra-processed" does not transfer cleanly.** In a large European cohort, the higher disease risk of ultra-processed food concentrated in animal-based products and sweetened drinks; plant-based alternatives showed no detectable association [\[9\]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10730313/).
- **Processing can still drive overeating.** In the one randomised trial that isolated processing, people ate about 500 kcal a day more on an ultra-processed diet without being asked to [\[10\]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7946062/). Nobody has run that experiment with a complete food.

The home-cooked vegan diet has a documented weakness of its own. In the EPIC-Oxford cohort, vegans had 2.31 times the hip-fracture rate of meat eaters [\[11\]](https://bmcmedicine.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12916-020-01815-3), and the association persisted after adjusting for calcium and protein intake, which points at whether the diet covers every nutrient rather than at plant food as such. A fortified product is designed to close exactly that gap. Whether it does in practice has never been tested.

## 6. Assessment

Environmental impact is not a good reason to choose either way of eating. The levers that move the number cut across both: avoiding heated-greenhouse produce out of season, not wasting food, not driving to the shop, and how the cooking is powered. Where the motivation is convenience, cost or reliable nutrient coverage, the environmental evidence neither supports nor blocks a complete food. Replacing some meals with it captures most of the waste and cooking advantage while keeping the variety of a mixed diet, whose long-term value is far better established than anything said here about powders.
